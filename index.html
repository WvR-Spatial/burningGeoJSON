Method & limitations

Data pipeline

A scheduled GitHub Action queries the NASA FIRMS Area API every six hours with a key held as a repository secret, normalises the differing MODIS and VIIRS schemas, clusters the detections, and commits static GeoJSON. The page fetches same-origin files and calls no external API at runtime — so it cannot be broken by a third-party proxy or an expired browser key.

What a detection is — and is not

Each point is a thermal anomaly: a satellite pixel whose mid-infrared signature exceeded a threshold at the moment of overpass. It is not a fire perimeter, and its position is the pixel centroid — VIIRS pixels are ~375 m across, MODIS ~1 km at nadir and considerably larger off-nadir. Gas flares, industrial heat and hot bare ground all produce detections.

Intensity

Colour encodes Fire Radiative Power (megawatts) on a log scale, not brightness temperature. FRP is a physical measure of the rate of energy release; brightness temperature saturates and varies with how much of the pixel the fire actually fills.

Why "cumulative FRP" and not a total

Each FRP value is an instantaneous rate, measured at the moment of overpass. Adding readings taken hours or days apart does not produce a physical total, because power does not sum across time — energy does. The quantity a fire scientist would report is Fire Radiative Energy, the integral of FRP over time, which requires assumptions about what interval each observation represents.

So the figures here are labelled honestly. Cumulative FRP is a relative activity index: valid for ranking one complex against another and for comparing one day against the next, not interpretable as energy released or area burnt. Peak detection is a genuine instantaneous measurement and is directly meaningful.

Clustering

Detections across the whole window are grouped with DBSCAN (eps = 1.5 km, minPts = 3). The 1.5 km radius links pixels up to roughly four VIIRS cells apart — the scale of a contiguous fire front — while minPts = 3 discards isolated single-pixel detections, which is where most false positives live. Clusters are drawn as convex hulls; a hull is an over-estimate of burnt area for a concave or ribbon-shaped fire.

Growth

Trend compares the latest day's detection count against the mean of the preceding days that complex was active — a ratio, so small and large fires are comparable. Detection counts are a proxy for activity, confounded by cloud cover, overpass timing and swath geometry: a fire can appear to "shrink" simply because cloud obscured the afternoon pass.

Known limits

  • Near-real-time data is provisional and is revised in the science-quality product weeks later.
  • Cloud blocks detection entirely; absence of points is not absence of fire.
  • Overpass cadence means small, short-lived fires between passes are missed.
  • Convex hull area is a footprint envelope, not a mapped burn scar.
